# Evaluation Methodology for Conflict-Aware RAG: Abstention, Multi-Answer Outputs, Attribution, and Majority-Wrong Traps (2023 – Nov 2026)

Scope note: These notes cover how published work scores (a) systems that abstain or report unresolved/multiple answers, (b) citation and attribution quality, (c) the split between retrieval failure and resolution failure including "majority wrong" behaviour, (d) answer matching under conflict, (e) ablation/significance practice and credible sample sizes, (f) user studies of evidence-inspection interfaces, and (g) known critiques. The target system emits one of four decisions (answered / unresolved_conflict / needs_information / insufficient_evidence) plus verified citations. Everything below is sourced; items I could not verify from a primary source are flagged in Gaps. Note on the brief: the paper the brief calls "The Art of Saying No" does not exist under that title; the abstention survey is "The Art of Refusal" (arXiv v1 title), later retitled "Know Your Limits: A Survey of Abstention in Large Language Models" (TACL) — [arXiv 2407.18418](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3).

## KQ1. How do knowledge-conflict, selective-prediction and abstention papers score a system that declines or reports multiple valid answers?

### Takeaway
There is a mature, confusion-matrix-based vocabulary for scoring abstention (abstention precision/recall/F1, coverage, reliable accuracy, effective reliability, AURCC/AUACC/AUROC, C@Acc) codified in the Know Your Limits survey, and a separate, stricter vocabulary for multi-answer conflict outputs (RAMDocs "all gold and no misinformation answers" EM; AmbigDocs five-way ontology + Entity-Answer Recall; WikiContradict correct/partially-correct/incorrect with a "both without preference" rule; CONFLICTS per-type "expected-behaviour adherence"). The closest published analogues to a four-way decision head are RefusalBench's refusal-category codes (REFUSE_CONTRADICTORY / REFUSE_AMBIGUOUS / REFUSE_MISSING, etc.) and CONFLICTS' five-way conflict-type prediction accuracy; both score "did the model pick the right decision class" separately from "was the answer correct".

### Cited Findings

**Abstention confusion matrix and derived metrics (Know Your Limits survey, Table 1 / §4.2)**
- The survey defines a 5-cell confusion matrix: N1 = correct answer, no abstention expected; N2 = incorrect answer, no abstention expected; N3 = abstained, no abstention expected; N4 = incorrect answer, abstention expected; N5 = abstained, abstention expected. Derived metrics (with the papers the survey credits): Abstention Accuracy = (N1+N5)/total (Feng et al. 2024b); Abstention Precision = N5/(N3+N5) (Feng et al. 2024b); Abstention Recall a.k.a. "Prudence Score" = N5/(N2+N4+N5) (Feng et al. 2024b; Cao et al. 2023; Varshney et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2023); Abstention F1 (Feng et al. 2024b); Coverage a.k.a. Acceptance Rate = (N1+N2+N4)/total (Cao et al. 2023); Abstention Rate = (N3+N5)/total; Over-conservativeness Score a.k.a. ARSP = N3/(N1+N2+N3) (Yang et al. 2023; Varshney et al. 2023); Reliable Accuracy R-Acc = N1/(N1+N2+N4) (Feng et al. 2024b); Effective Reliability ER = (N1−N2−N4)/total (Feng et al. 2024b; Si et al. 2023; Whitehead et al. 2022); Abstain-ECE (Feng et al. 2024b, building on Guo et al. 2017). — [Know Your Limits, arXiv 2407.18418v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3)
- Selective-prediction curve metrics in the same survey: Coverage@Acc (C@Acc) = maximum coverage C% such that accuracy on the C% most-confident predictions is ≥ Acc% (Cole et al. 2023; Si et al. 2023); Area Under Risk-Coverage Curve (AURCC) = average risk (error rate on retained items) over thresholds, lower is better (Si et al. 2023; Yoshikawa & Okazaki 2023); Area Under Accuracy-Coverage Curve (AUACC), higher is better (Cole et al. 2023; Xin et al. 2021); AUROC of the uncertainty score as a binary classifier of correctness (Cole et al. 2023; Kuhn et al. 2023). — [Know Your Limits](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3)
- The survey notes it does not give a formula for partial abstention; it suggests scoring partial abstention as a weighted sum of abstention and answer accuracy, and that LLM-as-judge is widely used for abstention detection ("judgments that agree well with humans but incur high financial and time costs"). — [Know Your Limits](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3)
- 2026 selective-prediction preprints argue AUROC (hallucination detection) and AURC (abstention policy quality) measure different things: "strong AUROC does not imply reliable selective prediction at strict safety thresholds"; one uses E-AURC (excess AURC over an oracle ranking). A constant confidence equal to empirical accuracy achieves ECE = 0 while giving no useful ranking for abstention, which is why ranking metrics are reported alongside calibration. — [arXiv 2605.22864](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.22864); [arXiv 2603.21172](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21172v1); [arXiv 2605.02915](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.02915) (all preprints; details secondhand from search snippets)

**R-Tuning (refusal-tuning) metrics**
- Accuracy = (# correctly answered) / (# willingly answered questions) (Eq. 3). Average Precision: rank predictions by confidence from high to low; AP = Σ_k (R(k+1) − R(k)) · P(k), where P(k) = #correct above threshold k / #answers above threshold k, R(k) = #correct above threshold k / #correct overall. Correctness = "the first few output tokens contain the ground-truth answer". Confidence for R-Tuning is a weighted combination of P("I am sure") and answer probability. The paper gives no formal rule for detecting refusals. — [R-Tuning, arXiv 2311.09677](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.09677)

**Grounded / RAG-specific refusal metrics**
- "Do RALMs Know When They Don't Know?" (AAAI 2026) defines Over-refusal rate = B/(A+B+C) (share of should-answer questions refused), Proper refusal rate = E/(D+E+F) (share of should-refuse questions refused), answer precision/recall/F1, refusal precision/recall/F1, Overall Accuracy OAcc = proportion of outputs that are either a correct answer or a proper refusal, and Brier score for calibration. Context settings are written ApBn (A positive, B negative chunks): no-context, 0p10n, 1p9n, 5p5n, 1p19n. Under 0p10n (all irrelevant), over-refusal rises from 0.355 (vanilla Qwen-2.5-7B) to 0.678 with R-Tuning; "improved refusal does not necessarily mean better calibration or higher accuracy" (e.g., ICFT(p) Brier 0.204 but refusal F1 0.000). Sampling: temperature 0.5, 16 generations. — [arXiv 2509.01476v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01476v3)
- RefusalBench (selective refusal in grounded QA) defines six perturbation categories each with an expected refusal code: Ambiguity (REFUSE_AMBIGUOUS), Contradiction (REFUSE_CONTRADICTORY), Missing information (REFUSE_MISSING), False premise (REFUSE_FALSE_PREMISE), Granularity mismatch (REFUSE_GRANULARITY), Epistemic mismatch (REFUSE_NONFACTUAL); each at LOW (should answer) / MEDIUM / HIGH (should refuse) intensity, 176 perturbation "levers". Metrics: refusal accuracy, answer accuracy, false refusal rate (over-refusal), missed refusal rate (under-refusal), refusal-detection F1, a composite Calibrated Refusal Score (CRS), and ECE reported separately for answers and refusals. A refusal counts as correct only if it matches the ground-truth category. Sizes: RefusalBench-NQ 1,600; RefusalBench-GaRAGe 1,506. Judge: Claude-4-Sonnet (answer correct at quality 4–5). Findings: best NQ refusal accuracy 73.0% (Claude-4-Sonnet), dropping to 36.1% on GaRAGe; no frontier model exceeds 80% on both answer and refusal accuracy; refusal ECE 0.226–0.519; models default to "missing information" (~25% of NQ predictions); "refusal behaves as two separable skills: detecting when to refuse and categorizing why". — [RefusalBench, arXiv 2510.10390](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10390v1)
- RGB (Chen et al., AAAI 2024) negative-rejection metric: only noisy documents are supplied and the model is told to output the string "I can not answer the question because of the insufficient information in documents"; Rej = exact-string match rate; Rej* = ChatGPT-judged rejection rate (because models often do not follow the string instruction, Rej and Rej* differ). — [RGB, arXiv 2309.01431v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.01431v2)

**Multi-answer / inter-context conflict scoring**
- RAMDocs/MADAM-RAG (COLM 2025): "an output is considered correct if and only if it includes all gold answers and no incorrect or misleading answers supported only by misinformation documents." RAMDocs = 500 queries from AmbigDocs, 1–3 correct answers each (avg 2.20), 1–3 supporting docs per answer (avg 3.84), 0–2 misinformation docs (entity-swapped), 0–2 noise docs; avg 5.53 docs per query, 1.70 misinformation/noise. The matching procedure (string vs judge) is not described. Best baseline Llama-3.3-70B prompt-based = 32.60 EM on RAMDocs; MADAM-RAG 34.40. The paper also reports precision/recall/F1 over answer sets in aggregator ablations. No seeds, CIs or significance tests are reported. — [arXiv 2504.13079v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13079v2)
- AmbigDocs (COLM 2024) five-way answer ontology: Complete (all valid answers, each paired with its disambiguated entity), Partial (some but not all, each paired with entity), Merged (multiple answers without entity names), Ambiguous (one valid answer without entity name), No Answer (abstains). Metrics: Answer Recall = (1/m) Σ R(a_i, y) (token-level recall of each gold answer in output y); Entity-Answer Recall (EAR) = (1/m) Σ R(e_i, y) · R(a_i, y) (answer counts only if its entity also appears); plus a DF1 score. Classification is a rule-based lexical heuristic with length-based token-recall thresholds; agreement with human labels κ = 0.83 on 500 outputs (inter-annotator κ = 0.85). Dataset: 36,098 examples (25,268/3,610/7,220), 2.92 answers/question. With gold documents only, no model exceeds EAR 0.5 (GPT-4 EAR 0.473). — [AmbigDocs, arXiv 2404.12447](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2404.12447)
- WikiContradict (NeurIPS 2024 D&B): five prompt templates (T1 closed-book; T2/T3 single passage; T4 two contradictory passages; T5 two contradictory passages + explicit instruction to consider conflicts). For T4/T5, Correct = surfaces both contradictory answers, does not prefer one, and flags that they conflict; Partially correct = matches only one answer, or matches both but favours one; Incorrect = matches none, or presents both as simultaneously possible without noting the conflict. Scale: 253 annotated instances; human eval of 1,375 responses (5 LLMs × 55 instances × 5 templates), two authors per response, final WikiContradict_HumanEval = 1,200 samples from 48 instances. Cohen's κ by template: T1 0.88, T2 0.85, T3 0.58, T4 0.67. Automatic judge WikiContradictEval = Llama-3-70B-Instruct few-shot, ~0.80 F-score (80.4 on the Correct class); GPT-4 judge 82.5. GPT-4 "Correct" rate under T5: 10.0% explicit / 11.8% implicit conflicts (10.4% overall); Llama-3-70B: 60.0% / 17.6% (43.8%). — [WikiContradict, arXiv 2406.13805v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13805v1)
- CONFLICTS (DRAGged into Conflicts, 2025): taxonomy with expected behaviour — No conflict (direct answer, no hedging); Complementary Information (consolidate, don't frame as debate); Conflicting opinions or research outcomes (reflect the debate, neutral summary); Conflict Due to Outdated Information / "Freshness" (prioritise up-to-date, optionally acknowledge outdated); Conflict Due to Misinformation (disregard inaccurate sources). 458 instances (161/115/115/62/5), ~9.2 search results per query, two expert annotators + reconciliation + third-expert review (no IAA reported). Metrics: (1) Factual grounding = share of response sentences an LLM labels supported; (2) Answer recall on the 228 single-gold-answer queries via LLM semantic-inclusion judge; (3) Expected-behaviour adherence = binary few-shot LLM judge with a per-category prompt, 0.89 accuracy vs humans on 100 examples; (4) Conflict-type prediction accuracy. Best conflict-type prediction: Gemini 2.5 Flash 65.3%. Oracle conflict type raises adherence ~24 points; predict-then-answer "Pipeline" prompting ~+9; taxonomy-aware prompt ~+5.5; residual errors are hedging on No-conflict/Freshness and single-viewpoint treatment of opinions. — [CONFLICTS, arXiv 2506.08500v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08500v2)
- The EMNLP 2024 knowledge-conflicts survey lists inter-context evaluation datasets (WikiContradiction 2,210; ClaimDiff 2,941; Pan et al. 2023a 52,189 on SQuAD; ContraDoc 449; ConflictingQA 238) and reports only "detection accuracy" as an inter-context metric, noting it is "subpar" for GPT-4/PaLM-2/Llama-2; it does not define a standard metric suite. — [Xu et al., arXiv 2403.08319](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08319); ACL Anthology [2024.emnlp-main.486](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.486/)
- MAGIC (2025) frames inter-context conflict as a conflict-detection task over knowledge-graph-derived multi-hop conflicts and reports that models "struggle with conflict detection—especially when multi-hop reasoning is required" and that little work examines fine-grained conflict types. — [MAGIC, arXiv 2507.21544](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21544v3)

### Inferences
- For the four-decision system, the most reviewer-legible design is a two-layer evaluation: (1) a decision-level 4×4 confusion matrix with per-class precision/recall/F1 and macro-F1 (directly analogous to RefusalBench's "categorize why" and CONFLICTS' conflict-type accuracy), plus the Know-Your-Limits aggregates where "abstain" = any of the three non-answer decisions (abstention precision/recall, coverage, R-Acc, ER); and (2) a content-level score conditioned on the decision: strict RAMDocs-style set EM (all gold, no misinformation-only answers) or answer-set P/R/F1 for `answered` and `unresolved_conflict`, and WikiContradict's "both, no preference, flags conflict" rubric for `unresolved_conflict`.
- Report risk-coverage/AURCC and C@Acc if the system exposes any confidence score; otherwise reviewers will ask why only a single operating point is reported.
- `needs_information` maps onto RefusalBench's REFUSE_AMBIGUOUS/REFUSE_GRANULARITY and AmbigDocs' ambiguity cases; `insufficient_evidence` onto REFUSE_MISSING / RGB negative rejection; `unresolved_conflict` onto REFUSE_CONTRADICTORY, WikiContradict T4/T5 and CONFLICTS "Conflicting opinions". Explicitly stating this mapping makes results comparable to prior work.
- RefusalBench's observation that models default to "missing information" means the `insufficient_evidence` class should be checked for over-prediction (confusion row) rather than only reported as a rate.

### Gaps
- Feng et al. 2024b ("Don't Hallucinate, Abstain", the source of R-Acc/ER/Abstain-ECE per the survey) was not fetched directly; definitions are cited via the survey.
- RefusalBench's Calibrated Refusal Score formula was not visible in the fetched text.
- RAMDocs does not disclose whether "includes all gold answers" is string containment or an LLM judge; check the GitHub repo (HanNight/RAMDocs) before claiming comparability.
- No paper found that proposes a named decision-level metric for a four-way (answer / conflict / clarify / insufficient) head; the field uses per-category accuracy (CONFLICTS, RefusalBench) rather than a single composite.

## KQ2. How is source attribution / citation quality evaluated, and which automatic judges are accepted?

### Takeaway
The accepted standard is ALCE's NLI-based citation recall and citation precision (Gao et al. 2023), built on AIS/AutoAIS (binary "attributable or not"); AttrScore adds a three-way attributable/extrapolatory/contradictory label and CAQA a four-way one. RAGBench/TRACe and RAGChecker provide sentence- or claim-level grounding metrics and show that fine-tuned NLI-style models (DeBERTa) beat zero-shot LLM judges on adherence. Exact-quote verification is done deterministically by substring match in practice, but no standard benchmark exists for it.

### Cited Findings
- ALCE (Gao et al., EMNLP 2023) citation recall: for each statement s_i, score 1 iff it has ≥1 citation and φ(concat(C_i), s_i) = 1, where φ is the TRUE NLI model (T5-11B) and concat(C_i) joins all cited passages; averaged over statements. Citation precision: a citation c_{i,j} is "irrelevant" iff (a) φ(c_{i,j}, s_i) = 0 and (b) φ(concat(C_i \ {c_{i,j}}), s_i) = 1; a citation scores 1 iff its statement has recall 1 and the citation is not irrelevant; averaged over citations. Statements are split at sentence boundaries (QAMPARI: each list entity is a statement). Correctness: EM recall (ASQA), recall-5 (QAMPARI), claim recall via InstructGPT sub-claims + TRUE (ELI5); fluency via MAUVE. Human agreement: accuracy 85.1% (recall) and 77.6% (precision); Cohen's κ 0.698 (recall, substantial) and 0.525 (precision, moderate). — [ALCE, arXiv 2305.14627](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.14627)
- AIS/AutoAIS: under AIS a statement is attributable if a human reading only the cited sources could verify it; AutoAIS automates this with an NLI model as a binary judgment; Bohnet et al. 2022 (Attributed QA) use binary attributable/not. — [LLM Sourcing survey, arXiv 2510.10161](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.10161); [AttrScore, arXiv 2305.06311](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.06311); [Attributed QA, arXiv 2212.08037](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.08037v2.pdf)
- AttrScore (Yue et al. 2023): three labels — attributable (reference fully supports), extrapolatory ("the reference lacks sufficient information to support the generated statement"), contradictory ("the generated answer contradicts the information presented in the reference"). — [AttrScore](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.06311)
- CAQA (2024) argues AttrScore's three-way and AutoAIS's binary labels are too coarse and proposes four: supportive, insufficient, contradictory, irrelevant, built from knowledge graphs. — [CAQA, arXiv 2401.14640](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.14640v1)
- ALiiCE extends ALCE to positional fine-grained citations and treats a citation as redundant if it adds no additional valid support. — [ALiiCE review](https://www.themoonlight.io/review/aliice-evaluating-positional-fine-grained-citation-generation)
- CiteEval (2025) is a principle-driven citation evaluation framework that, per a secondary account, extends AutoAIS-style recall to precision by assessing each citation's relevance. — [CiteEval, arXiv 2506.01829](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2506.01829) (details secondhand)
- RAGBench/TRACe (2024): Relevance = Len(R_i)/Len(d_i) (relevant spans over document length, retriever quality); Utilization = Len(U_i)/Len(d_i) (used spans, generator); Completeness = Len(R_i ∩ U_i)/Len(R_i); Adherence = example-level boolean "all response parts grounded in context" (span-level supported tokens also kept). Ground-truth labels by GPT-4 (gpt-4-0125-preview) with CoT, re-annotated up to 3× on inconsistency (<2% residual). Evaluation: AUROC for adherence/hallucination, RMSE for relevance/utilization. Fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-Large reaches AUROC 0.64–0.87 vs LLM judges (GPT-3.5 zero-shot 0.51–0.65; TruLens 0.40–0.70; RAGAS ≤~0.70). Agreement with DelucionQA human labels: adherence F1 0.96. Abstract says RoBERTa, body says DeBERTa (inconsistency). — [RAGBench, arXiv 2407.11005v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11005v2)
- RAGChecker (2024): claim-level precision = share of response claims that are correct; recall = share of ground-truth claims the response covers; F1 harmonic mean; retriever claim recall = fraction of ground-truth claims found in retrieved chunks; retriever precision is chunk-level (so claim-level retriever precision cannot reach 100%). Meta-evaluated against 10 metrics from TruLens, RAGAS, ARES, CRUD-RAG. — [RAGChecker, arXiv 2408.08067](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.08067)
- ARES (2023): scores context relevance, answer faithfulness, answer relevance with fine-tuned LLM judges, and uses prediction-powered inference (PPI) to produce 95% confidence intervals from a small annotated set (secondary sources say ~150 human labels) plus many unlabeled predictions; systems ranked by interval midpoint. — [ARES, arXiv 2311.09476](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09476v2)
- CONFLICTS' factual-grounding metric: an LLM labels each response sentence supported / unsupported / contradictory / non-factual; score = share supported. — [CONFLICTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08500v2)
- Exact-quote verification: practitioner write-ups verify quotes via substring match against source text rather than another model, noting models asked to quote verbatim sometimes produce near-quotes "with a word swapped or a number nudged". CoVeGAT (RANLP 2025 workshop) built 500 genuine claim–quote pairs and 500 minimally perturbed false variants; a MiniLM baseline reached 96.4% accuracy. A 2026 clinical preprint reports a model producing verbatim quotes for 98.0% of claims but fully substantiating only 37.1% (snippet-level, unverified). No standard exact-quote benchmark was found. — [Vaquill engineering note](https://www.vaquill.ai/engineering/verify-ai-citations-in-code); [CoVeGAT, ACL Anthology 2025.r2lm-1.1](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2025.r2lm-1.1); [arXiv 2609.15964 (HF page)](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.15964); [Verifiable by Design / QUOTE-TUNING, arXiv 2404.03862](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.03862)
- BERGEN (Findings of EMNLP 2024) argues surface-matching metrics (EM, F1, Rouge-L) are insufficient for RAG and recommends LLM-based semantic evaluation. — [BERGEN, arXiv 2407.01102](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01102v1); [ACL Anthology 2024.findings-emnlp.449](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.449.pdf)

### Inferences
- For "verified citations" the credible protocol is layered: (i) deterministic exact-quote check (normalised substring match against the chunk text) reported as quote-verification rate; (ii) ALCE citation recall/precision with an NLI model (TRUE / DeBERTa-NLI) for statements that are not verbatim; (iii) a small human-audited subset (≥100 statements) reporting κ against the automatic judge, mirroring ALCE's 85.1/77.6 accuracy reporting and CONFLICTS' 0.89-on-100 validation.
- A "gold document selected" metric (did the cited chunk ID equal the annotated gold chunk) is a retrieval-side metric and is best reported alongside RAGChecker's retriever claim recall or TRACe Relevance, not in place of NLI-based citation recall.
- Reviewers accept fine-tuned NLI/DeBERTa judges and GPT-4-class judges for attribution; RAGBench's evidence that zero-shot GPT-3.5/RAGAS/TruLens underperform a fine-tuned DeBERTa argues against relying on an off-the-shelf zero-shot LLM judge alone for adherence.

### Gaps
- RAGAS's exact faithfulness/answer-relevancy/context-precision definitions were not fetched (search returned no primary text); cite the RAGAS paper (arXiv 2309.15217) directly if needed.
- GaRAGe's RAF score (used by RefusalBench-GaRAGe) was not fetched.
- Bohnet et al. 2022 AutoAIS definition is cited via secondary papers, not the primary text.
- CiteEval details are from a secondary page.

## KQ3. How do papers decompose retrieval failure vs. resolution failure, and is there a standard name for "the majority wrong answer was chosen"?

### Takeaway
Decomposition is done by (a) separate retriever-side metrics (recall@k, RAGChecker retriever claim recall, TRACe Relevance) versus generator-side metrics (claim precision/recall, Utilization, Completeness, Adherence), and (b) controlled "gold-only" vs "gold + distractors" vs "no gold" context conditions. There is no single standard name for majority-wrong behaviour: the closest named quantities are Longpre's memorization ratio M_R = p_o/(p_o+p_s), Xie et al.'s MR = f_m/(f_m+f_c) with controlled evidence counts, ClashEval's context bias / prior bias / context preference rate, Jin et al.'s "majority rule" finding, and RGB's error detection / error correction rates. "Misinformation pollution" (Pan et al. 2023) is a threat framing, not a metric name.

### Cited Findings
- RAGChecker splits retriever metrics (claim recall over retrieved chunks; chunk-level precision) from generator metrics (claim precision/recall/F1), explicitly to diagnose which component fails. — [RAGChecker](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.08067)
- TRACe assigns Relevance to the retriever and Utilization/Completeness/Adherence to the generator. — [RAGBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11005v2)
- Longpre et al. 2021 use Recall@K (retrieved passage contains gold answer) for retrieval quality and the memorization ratio for reader behaviour; M_R = p_o/(p_o+p_s) where p_o = fraction of substituted examples where the model predicts the Original (parametric) answer and p_s = fraction predicting the Substitute (context) answer; computed only on examples the model answered correctly on the unaltered input. Substitution types: Corpus Substitution, Type Swap, Popularity, Alias (not a true conflict). Findings: memorization rises with model size (from <15% to ≥50%); numeric→textual substitutions yield ≥83% Original predictions. — [Longpre et al., arXiv 2109.05052v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2109.05052v2)
- Xie et al. (ICLR 2024) define MR = f_m/(f_m+f_c) over a multiple-choice format (memory answer / counter-answer / "Uncertain"); with controlled evidence counts on PopQA, ChatGPT's MR rises from 3.7% (0 of 2 pieces support memory) to 99.8% (2 of 2); GPT-4 reaches ~80% MR on the most popular entities; order effects >5% for most models and >30% for PaLM2/Llama2-7B. — [Xie et al., arXiv 2305.13300](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.13300)
- Jin et al. (Tug-of-War, COLING 2024) report that RALMs "lean toward placing trust in evidence that appears more frequently" (majority rule), plus confirmation bias (favour memory-consistent evidence), availability bias, and a Dunning-Kruger effect (stronger models favour faulty memory); propose CD2 contrastive decoding. — [Jin et al., arXiv 2402.14409](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14409) (abstract only; metric definitions not extracted)
- ClashEval (NeurIPS 2024 D&B) defines Accuracy = P(correct | context or prior is correct); Prior bias = P(uses prior | context correct, prior wrong); Context bias = P(uses context | context wrong, prior correct); Context preference rate = proportion of responses aligned with the context. Perturbations: numeric multipliers 0.1–10×, date shifts ±100y, categorical slight/significant/comical. ~1,294 questions, six domains (per-domain counts sum to 1,278 — inconsistency). Claude Opus accuracy 0.743 / context bias 0.157 / prior bias 0.021; GPT-4o 0.615 / 0.304 / 0.021. Calibrated token-probability correction raises GPT-4o to 0.754 accuracy, context bias 0.107. — [ClashEval, arXiv 2404.10198v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10198v2)
- RGB counterfactual robustness: documents with injected factual errors; model instructed to output "There are factual errors in the provided documents"; ED = exact-string error-detection rate; ED* = ChatGPT-judged; CR = error correction rate (correct answer produced after detecting error; denominator not stated). Only models with >70% closed-book accuracy are included so that the counterfactual test measures conflict handling rather than knowledge gaps. Noise robustness: 5 documents, noise ratios 0/0.2/0.4/0.6/0.8. 600 base questions (+200 integration, +200 counterfactual), half English/half Chinese. — [RGB](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.01431v2)
- RAMDocs varies supporting-document imbalance (200 examples, one answer fixed at 1 doc, other 1–3; baseline falls "by up to 8%") and misinformation count (200 queries, 1–3 misinformation docs; concatenated-prompt and Astute RAG baselines each drop 46%). — [RAMDocs](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13079v2)
- Pan et al. (EMNLP 2023) frame "misinformation pollution" of ODQA corpora and test prompting, misinformation detection and majority voting as defences; Pan et al. (AACL 2023) show QA models are vulnerable to small amounts of contaminated evidence. Neither names a dedicated metric. — [arXiv 2305.13661](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13661); [arXiv 2110.07803](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.07803)
- Later pollution protocols: MIRAGE uses Clean / Mixed (50% polluted) / Full (100%) regimes and cross-document consistency as a signal; a 2026 preprint proposes "parametric override" as a metric of drift from correct parametric knowledge under misleading context; RA-RAG uses reliability-weighted majority voting; CrAM uses QA accuracy under low-credibility documents. — [MIRAGE, arXiv 2607.05069](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05069); [arXiv 2606.07783](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07783v1); [RA-RAG, arXiv 2410.22954](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.22954v5); [CrAM, arXiv 2406.11497](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2406.11497) (all from snippets)
- A 2026 reward-shaping paper isolates reader from retriever by evaluating three conditions: evidence removed, gold evidence only, gold + distractors, with "tagged accuracy". — [Rajapakse et al. 2026](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/m.derijke/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/rajapakse-2026-reward.pdf) (snippet)
- Authority bias: an ACL 2025 paper shows LLMs favour one knowledge source when user-provided and retrieved content conflict and proposes conflict detection + credibility assessment (CDEQ). — [ACL 2025 long 1400](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2025.acl-long.1400)

### Inferences
- A credible decomposition for the target pipeline: report (1) gold-chunk recall@k; (2) conditional decision accuracy given gold-in-context (accuracy | gold retrieved) and given gold-not-retrieved — this isolates resolution failure, following RGB's closed-book filter and the gold-only/gold+distractor convention; (3) a majority-wrong rate defined explicitly as P(final answer = majority-supported wrong answer | majority of retrieved chunks support a wrong answer and ≥1 chunk supports the correct one), and name it transparently (e.g., "majority-trap rate") while stating it is analogous to ClashEval's context bias and Xie et al.'s MR under controlled evidence counts; do not claim it is a standard term.
- Report the sycophancy-to-evidence curve as a function of the fraction of wrong-supporting documents (Xie et al.'s 0/2, 1/2, 2/2 design; RAMDocs' 1–3 misinformation docs), since reviewers in this area expect a dose–response plot rather than a single rate.

### Gaps
- No paper found that standardises the name "majority-trap rate", "misinformation pollution rate" or "sycophancy-to-evidence" as a metric; these appear only as informal descriptions.
- Jin et al.'s evaluation-framework dimensions and formulae were not extracted (abstract only).
- No source found that reports "gold-in-context conditional accuracy" under that exact label; it is a common analysis pattern rather than a named metric.

## KQ4. Standard practice for answer matching in open QA under conflict, and known pitfalls

### Takeaway
Lexical EM/containment remains the default in conflict benchmarks (RGB, RAMDocs, AmbigDocs, R-Tuning) but is known to underestimate correctness by ~20 points on NQ-open (Kamalloo et al. 2023), with ~50% of EM failures being semantic equivalents and ~21% list-style answers — exactly the cases that dominate multi-answer conflict outputs. GPT-4-class judges reach Kendall τ ≈ 0.79 with humans on short answers but misjudge ~3% of unattributable answers and are not trusted for long-form outputs; verbosity bias in LLM judges is documented, and containment is itself length-sensitive.

### Cited Findings
- Kamalloo et al. (ACL 2023), 301 NQ-open questions, 12 systems: EM underestimates human-judged accuracy by 12.9 to 58.8 points (average +24 for human, +21 InstructGPT-eval, +16 BEM). Of 493 EM false negatives judged correct by humans: semantic equivalence 50.3%, list-style questions 20.6%, granularity discrepancies 15.0%, data quality ~14%. Inter-annotator Fleiss' κ 72.8% (NQ-open), 83.5% (CuratedTREC). GPT-4-eval reduces EM failures by 72%, trails human accuracy by 3.3 points, Kendall τ 0.79 with humans, ~3% error on unattributable answers; InstructGPT-eval τ 0.75, ~10% error; BEM τ 0.70 and misranks the best system. Recommendations: use regex for syntactic variants; zero-shot LLM judging is a reasonable substitute for short answers; automated judges cannot detect unattributable long-form answers; "human evaluation has no substitute". — [Kamalloo et al., arXiv 2305.06984](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.06984)
- RGB: "a response is correct if the generated text contains an exact match to the answer" (containment); rejection and error detection use fixed strings plus a ChatGPT judge because models often don't emit the strings. — [RGB](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.01431v2)
- AmbigDocs: rule-based token-recall thresholds; κ 0.83 vs humans; Ambiguous answers sometimes misclassified as Partial due to plurals/spelling variants. — [AmbigDocs](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2404.12447)
- R-Tuning: correct iff "the first few output tokens contain the ground-truth answer". — [R-Tuning](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.09677)
- WikiContradict uses a few-shot Llama-3-70B judge (F ≈ 0.80) because the "both answers, no preference, flags conflict" rubric cannot be string-matched; κ drops to 0.58 for T3 and 0.67 for T4, showing the conflict templates are harder even for humans. — [WikiContradict](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13805v1)
- CONFLICTS validates its LLM judge on 100 examples (0.89 accuracy); RefusalBench uses Claude-4-Sonnet with a 1–5 quality score (≥4 = correct) and category matching for refusals. — [CONFLICTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08500v2); [RefusalBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10390v1)
- LLM-judge pitfalls: the LLMs-as-Judges survey defines verbosity bias as favouring longer responses regardless of quality and describes CALM, a perturbation framework to measure it; "Judging the Judges" finds judges sensitive to prompt length/quality and sometimes failing even verbatim-match checks; a 2026 large-scale study found low verbosity bias for 21 judges on MT-Bench but warns against generalising. — [arXiv 2412.05579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.05579); [arXiv 2406.12624](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12624); [arXiv 2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19544)
- BERGEN recommends LLM-based semantic evaluation over EM/F1/Rouge-L for RAG. — [BERGEN](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01102v1)

### Inferences
- For a conflict benchmark where the target output is an answer set plus a decision label, the defensible matching protocol is: normalised containment with alias lists for atomic answers (as in RGB/AmbigDocs), a rubric-based LLM judge only for the decision-dependent properties (acknowledges conflict, does not prefer one side, pairs answer with its source), and a human-labelled calibration subset with κ reported — because the published κ for conflict-template judgments (0.58–0.67) is markedly lower than for single-answer templates (0.85–0.88).
- Containment is length-sensitive: longer outputs are more likely to contain a gold string (and, in RAMDocs' strict rule, also more likely to contain a misinformation string). Report answer length by decision class and, if using an LLM judge, randomise candidate order and hide system identity.

### Gaps
- No study found that quantifies verbosity/length bias specifically for conflict-QA judges or for containment scoring; the containment–length interaction is an inference.
- No published inter-annotator statistics found for CONFLICTS.

## KQ5. Ablation designs, significance tests, seeds, and credible sample sizes (30 → 1,000 questions)

### Takeaway
Current conflict benchmarks report single point estimates without CIs (RAMDocs) and are mostly in the 250–1,600 item range (WikiContradict 253 instances / 1,200 judgments; CONFLICTS 458; RAMDocs 500; RGB 600+400; ClashEval ~1,294; RefusalBench 1,600). Statistical guidance (Miller 2024; Card et al. 2020; Dror et al. 2018; Berg-Kirkpatrick et al. 2012) converges on: report SE/95% CI per metric, use paired comparisons on the same questions, cluster SEs when questions share a source document, and plan sample size by power analysis — roughly 1,000 independent questions to detect a 3-point difference at 80% power. A 30-question set yields a ±18-point binomial CI and can only support qualitative/pilot claims.

### Cited Findings
- Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals" (2024): SE = √(Var(s)/n), binary case √(s̄(1−s̄)/n); 95% CI = s̄ ± 1.96·SE; bootstrapping is "unnecessary in most cases" because the CLT applies; clustered SE = √(SE²_CLT + (1/n²) Σ_c Σ_i Σ_{j≠i}(s_ic − s̄)(s_jc − s̄)) for questions sharing a passage/source, with clustered SEs up to ~3× naive; paired SE for two models = √(SE_A² + SE_B² − 2·SE_A·SE_B·Corr(s_A,s_B)); resampling each question K times reduces per-question variance σ²/K; power formula n = (z_{α/2}+z_β)²·(ω² + σ_A²/K_A + σ_B²/K_B)/δ² with ω² = Var(x_A)+Var(x_B)−2Cov; worked example: ω² = 1/9, α = 0.05, power 0.8, δ = 0.03 → ≈969 questions, hence the suggestion that new evals have ≥~1,000 questions; with n = 198, raising K from 1 to 10 shrinks the minimum detectable effect from 13.2 to 7.5 points. Do not lower temperature to reduce variance. — [Miller, arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1)
- Card et al. (EMNLP 2020): "underpowered experiments are common in the NLP literature"; on GLUE "most attempted comparisons to state of the art models will not be adequately powered"; typical MT test sets of 2,000 sentences have ~75% power to detect 1 BLEU; typical human-rating designs are underpowered for small differences; releases power-analysis notebooks. — [Card et al., arXiv 2010.06595](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06595)
- Dror et al. (ACL 2018) survey finds significance testing often ignored or misused in ACL/TACL 2017 papers and propose a selection protocol; for paired data "one should use the paired version of the statistical significance test (such as the matched-pair t-test)"; appendix tables recommend bootstrap/permutation for non-normal metrics. — [Dror et al., ACL P18-1128](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/); [appendix arXiv 1809.01448](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.01448)
- Berg-Kirkpatrick et al. (2012) use the paired bootstrap, find doubling the test set does not remove the need for testing, and that significance predictions weaken across domains; a 2014 workshop paper uses the bootstrap only when sample size >200; course notes recommend 10k–100k resamples. — [Berg-Kirkpatrick et al. 2012](https://nlp.cs.berkeley.edu/pubs/BergKirkpatrick-Burkett-Klein_2012_Significance_paper.pdf); [ACL 2014 W14-1601](https://mirror.aclweb.org/acl2014/W14-16/pdf/W14-1601.pdf); [UMass sigtest notes](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~brenocon/anlp2018/lectures/11-sigtest.pdf)
- "Please, Don't Forget the Difference and the Confidence Interval" (2022) recommends reporting CIs alongside p-values because large samples make tiny p-values regardless of effect size. — [arXiv 2205.11134](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.11134)
- ARES obtains 95% CIs via prediction-powered inference from ~150 human-annotated points plus unlabeled predictions and ranks systems by interval midpoint; reported coverage >95% (secondary). — [ARES](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09476v2)
- Observed practice in conflict papers: RAMDocs reports single point estimates with no seeds/CIs; ablations vary number of debate rounds and aggregator (P/R/F1); analyses use 200-example subsets. — [RAMDocs](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13079v2). The AAAI 2026 RALM-refusal paper samples 16 generations at temperature 0.5 per question. — [arXiv 2509.01476v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01476v3). CONFLICTS ablates prompting regimes (Vanilla / Taxonomy-Aware / Pipeline / Oracle conflict type) and validates the judge on 100 items. — [CONFLICTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08500v2). WikiContradict human-evaluates 1,200 responses from 48 instances with two annotators and reports κ per template. — [WikiContradict](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13805v1). RGB restricts counterfactual tests to models with >70% closed-book accuracy. — [RGB](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.01431v2)

### Inferences
- Applying Miller's binary SE: at n = 30 with p ≈ 0.5, SE ≈ 0.091 (95% CI ≈ ±18 points); at n = 100, ±10 points; at n = 500, ±4.4 points; at n = 1,000, ±3.1 points. A 30-question pack is therefore a pilot/diagnostic artefact; paired comparison and K-fold resampling (K = 5–10 generations per question at the deployment temperature) can roughly halve the detectable effect but cannot make 30 items reviewer-credible for headline claims. The 250–500 range (WikiContradict, CONFLICTS, RAMDocs) is the de facto floor for published conflict benchmarks.
- Expected ablations for a conflict-aware pipeline, judged from what the cited papers do: (i) no-retrieval / closed-book baseline (RGB, WikiContradict T1); (ii) vanilla RAG concatenation (RAMDocs, CONFLICTS Vanilla); (iii) remove the conflict-detection or decision component; (iv) oracle conflict type / oracle gold chunks (CONFLICTS Oracle) to bound resolution-vs-retrieval; (v) dose–response over number/ratio of wrong-supporting documents (Xie, RAMDocs, RGB noise ratios); (vi) judge-robustness check with a second judge model or human subset.
- Because conflict questions are typically generated in groups from one source document or entity (RAMDocs: multiple answers per query; AmbigDocs: entities), clustered SEs by source/question-group are needed or SEs will be under-stated by up to ~3× (Miller).

### Gaps
- No venue-level statement found that specifies a minimum number of test questions reviewers require; the ~1,000 figure is Miller's power-analysis recommendation, not a reviewer policy.
- Card et al. details beyond the abstract (specific SQuAD/MRPC numbers) were not fetched.

## KQ6. User-study designs for evidence-inspection or conflict-resolution interfaces

### Takeaway
Published studies are small-N (26–60) within-subjects lab/online studies or larger (≈257) preregistered crowdsourced studies; measures are trust scales (TXAI), reliance via edit distance, confidence, verification behaviour (clicks/hovers/source views), task accuracy on factual questions, time (often logged but not always reported), and workload/usability (NASA-TLX, SUPR-Q). Findings are mixed: claim-level provenance can lower trust without changing behaviour (PaperTrail), citations raise trust even when irrelevant, and result-set bias shifts user accuracy from 43% to 23% (incorrect-biased) or 65% (correct-biased).

### Cited Findings
- PaperTrail (CHI '26): within-subjects, N = 26 analysed (38 recruited, 12 excluded), research staff at NASA centres; two tasks (multi-paper synthesis; devil's-advocate review) editing a ~300-word LLM draft over four papers; conditions: claim-evidence matching with coverage/omitted-claim cards vs. sentence-level citation baseline, counterbalanced. Measures: TXAI trust (1–7), reliance = normalised token-level Levenshtein similarity between draft and final text, confidence (2 items, α = 0.89), NASA-TLX, SUPR-Q, clicks, message counts, logged time-on-task (not reported). Tests: Shapiro-Wilk then paired t or Wilcoxon, two-tailed α = .05; Spearman for exploratory. Results: trust lower with PaperTrail (4.22→3.68; t(25) = 2.61, p = .015, d = 0.44); reliance unchanged (0.73 vs 0.75, p = .313); confidence unchanged; usability lower (p = .026); clicks higher but n.s. Accuracy was not measured. — [PaperTrail, arXiv 2602.21045](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.21045v1)
- Pogacar et al. (ICTIR 2017): lab study biasing medical search results toward correct or incorrect information; accuracy fell from 43% to 23% with incorrect-biased results and rose to 65% with correct-biased results; self-reported knowledge moderated the effect. — [Pogacar et al. 2017](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~aghenai/assets/publications/pogacar-ictir2017.pdf)
- Cau & Tintarev (2024): preregistered study with 257 participants on stance diversity and stance labels in search results, including possible backfire effects. — [arXiv 2401.11201](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.11201v1)
- Novin & Meyers (CAIS 2016): exploratory study finding document order and genre in search results influence users' understanding of a controversy. — [CAIS](https://journals.library.ualberta.ca/ojs.cais-acsi.ca/index.php/cais-asci/article/view/957)
- Allen et al. (TU Delft 2023): time pressure "considerably" affects task performance in search. — [TU Delft](https://research.tudelft.nl/files/167071511/978_3_031_34444_2_16.pdf)
- CIKM 2025 workshop paper: presence of citations elevates perceived trust even when the source is irrelevant; a CEUR workshop study found a "verifiable" condition yielded higher trust ratings, more citation hovers and more source views; a survey study (N = 104) on factuality/attribution cues frames trust calibration. A widely repeated claim that an AAAI 2025 study found random citations increase trust while opening citations decreases it could not be traced to a primary source. — [CIKM workshop (chatpaper summary)](https://chatpaper.com/paper/161941); [CEUR Vol-4265 paper 4](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4265/paper4.pdf); [arXiv 2405.20434](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2405.20434); [blog (secondary)](https://shop.zimaspace.com/blogs/tech-ai-hub/rag-answers-feel-confident-when-citations-hidden)
- "Relevant Is Not Warranted" (2026) argues cited RAG commits "citation laundering" — a related source presented as warrant for an over-strong claim — motivating evidence-force calibration. — [arXiv 2605.28044](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.28044)

### Inferences
- A conflict-inspection interface study that reviewers would recognise: within-subjects, N ≥ 24–30 (PaperTrail scale) or preregistered crowdsourced N ≈ 150–250 (Cau & Tintarev scale); tasks with known ground truth so accuracy can be measured (Pogacar design), with conditions = (baseline answer + citations) vs (decision label + inspectable conflicting evidence); measures = accuracy, time, TXAI trust, calibration of trust (trust in correct vs incorrect outputs, i.e., appropriate reliance), verification actions (opens/hovers), and NASA-TLX; tests = paired t / Wilcoxon with effect sizes, as in PaperTrail.
- Because PaperTrail found a trust–behaviour gap, report behavioural reliance (edit distance, override rate) alongside self-reported trust.

### Gaps
- No user study found that specifically evaluates a four-way decision display (answered / unresolved / needs info / insufficient) or an "unresolved conflict" output; evidence is adjacent (provenance interfaces, biased search results).
- Time-on-task results for PaperTrail are not reported despite being logged.
- The AAAI 2025 "random citations increase trust" study is unverified.

## KQ7. Known critiques: synthetic conflicts too easy, templated authority phrases, evaluation leakage, judge bias

### Takeaway
The best-documented critique is that entity-substitution conflicts are incoherent and therefore easy to ignore (Xie et al.), and that synthetic datasets exaggerate context properties rare in real retrieval (Hagström et al. DRUID; Xu et al. survey). Leakage is handled by filtering to items the model already knows (RGB >70% rule; survey note) and by using post-cutoff or fictional entities. Authority/credibility cues are known to be followed as surface signals ("authority bias"), which makes templated authority phrases circular if the same template is used in evaluation; judge bias (verbosity, string-based refusal detection) is documented.

### Cited Findings
- Xie et al.: word-level entity substitution "produces incoherent counter-memory" (e.g., Washington D.C.→London while surrounding text still points to the original); "Models can detect this mismatch and ignore the evidence"; generation-based coherent counter-memory (ChatGPT-written, NLI-checked with DeBERTa-v2, 99% manual accuracy on 200) makes models "highly receptive"; 34 of 50 "stubborn" cases traced to ambiguous counter-memory, commonsense or suggestive questions. — [Xie et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.13300)
- Hagström et al. (Reality Check / DRUID): "artificial datasets often fail to represent the complexity and diversity of realistically retrieved context"; synthetic datasets (CounterFact, ConflictQA) "exaggerate context characteristics that are rare in real retrieved data", inflating measured context utilisation; DRUID = real queries with retrieved contexts manually annotated for stance; on DRUID single context properties correlate weakly with utilisation (ACU score) and source-related properties matter more. — [arXiv 2412.17031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17031)
- Xu et al. survey: conflict creation "predominantly relies on the artificial generation" of misleading information; findings "might not easily transfer to practical applications"; calls for assessing "how well LLMs perform in real-world scenarios rather than artificially created conflicts" and notes current work "predominantly emphasizes evaluating their performance on common QA datasets". On leakage: "selecting a subset of the dataset that aligns with the tested model's knowledge is crucial" for context-memory datasets. — [Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08319)
- RGB includes only models with >70% closed-book accuracy in counterfactual tests, and its string-based Rej/ED metrics diverge from judge-based Rej*/ED* because models ignore the output-string instruction. — [RGB](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.01431v2)
- Longpre: memorization is "much lower on examples without answer overlap with training data", i.e., results on seen data overstate context-ignoring behaviour. — [Longpre](https://arxiv.org/html/2109.05052v2)
- Authority/credibility cues: ACL 2025 "LLMs Trust Humans More" documents authority bias in RAG; a 2026 preprint finds conflict resolution follows a "highly consistent source credibility hierarchy" and that repeating low-credibility claims shifts preference; AuthorityBench (2026, alphaxiv) tests authority perception with 10-/5-level labels and shows authority-guided filtering boosts accuracy; Credibility-Aware Generation (CAG) injects relevance/timeliness/source-reliability labels into the input. — [ACL 2025 long 1400](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2025.acl-long.1400); [arXiv 2601.03746](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.03746v1); [AuthorityBench, arXiv 2603.25092](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.25092); [CAG, arXiv 2404.06809](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.06809)
- Judge bias: verbosity bias (LLMs-as-Judges survey, CALM); prompt-length sensitivity ("Judging the Judges"); RefusalBench models default to the "missing information" category; AmbigDocs lexical classifier confuses Ambiguous with Partial; WikiContradict human κ falls to 0.58–0.67 on conflict templates. — [arXiv 2412.05579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.05579); [arXiv 2406.12624](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12624); [RefusalBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10390v1); [AmbigDocs](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2404.12447); [WikiContradict](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13805v1)
- Conflict-type imbalance: CONFLICTS has only 5 Misinformation instances of 458, so per-type scores on that class are unreliable. — [CONFLICTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08500v2)
- Multi-answer gold incompleteness: Kamalloo find 20.6% of EM "failures" are list-style questions with incomplete gold sets. — [Kamalloo et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.06984)

### Inferences
- Templated authority phrases ("according to the official regulation…") are circular when the same template both injects credibility and is the cue the system is trained/prompted to detect; the authority-bias and credibility-hierarchy findings show models already respond to such surface cues, so a test with templated phrases measures template recognition rather than credibility reasoning. The remedy implied by DRUID/Xie is naturalistic, LLM-written or real-retrieved sources with annotated stance and source metadata, plus an ablation that strips or shuffles authority cues to show the decision is not purely cue-driven.
- Leakage controls expected by reviewers: a closed-book baseline per item (RGB), reporting results separately for items the model answers correctly closed-book vs not (Longpre's "answered correctly on unaltered input" filter), and fictional/post-cutoff entities for synthetic conflicts.
- Synthetic-conflict sets should report a coherence check (NLI entailment of each document to its intended answer, as Xie et al. do at 99%) and a realism audit (fraction of documents a human annotator would judge plausible), otherwise the "too easy" critique applies.

### Gaps
- No paper found that explicitly critiques "templated authority phrases" as circular; this is inferred from the authority-bias and credibility-hierarchy literature.
- AuthorityBench and the 2026 credibility-hierarchy preprint details come from alphaxiv/snippets, not primary text.
- No paper found that quantifies evaluation leakage specifically for inter-context (document-vs-document) conflicts, as opposed to context-memory conflicts.
